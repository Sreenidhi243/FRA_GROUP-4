Page 01 · QUESTION

Would your risk label have survived a crash?

Every portfolio manager calls one book "safe" and another "aggressive" using today's data. This project tests whether that call would have held up in a crash — and whether today's allocation would still be optimal if the efficient frontier were rebuilt from a different era's numbers.

🎯 Objective

Test 1 – the risk label: compute 95% VaR and Expected Shortfall for both portfolios in a crisis and a calm period. Did "high risk" behave like high risk, and "low risk" hold up?
Test 2 – the allocation: take today's return as the target, rebuild the efficient frontier on each historical period, and ask whether the optimiser would still choose today's weights.

How we divided the 28 stocks — and why

The rule, step by step

1 · Lens = annualised volatility. VaR, ES and Markowitz are all built on the same quantity — the dispersion of returns — so the label ("high/low risk") is judged on the same yardstick as the tests that follow. Ratings and market cap were not in the supplied price files, so volatility is the primary lens. It is backed by four further numbers: beta against the Nifty 50 (market sensitivity — the standard textbook risk lens), maximum drawdown, excess kurtosis (fat tails) and average pair correlation.
2 · Rank all 28 eligible stocks by full-period (2020–26) volatility.
3 · Top 10 → A. Remove them. 4 · Lowest 10 of the remaining 18 → B. Picking A first and deleting it guarantees zero overlap. We take the two ends of the ranking (not the middle) so the contrast between the portfolios is large enough to test.
5 · Equal weights, 10 stocks each. 10 is the class minimum and equal weight is the current portfolio being evaluated — a deliberately naïve benchmark that the optimiser can try to beat.

Is the label backed by numbers? (not vibes)

Every row points the same way: A's stocks are individually more volatile, more market-sensitive (higher beta), more fat-tailed, more correlated and fell further peak-to-trough.

The targets and the measurement window

These are the supplied current returns of the two equal-weight portfolios over the last year (1 Oct 2025 – 30 Sep 2026), taken exactly as given in current_targets.csv. Test 2 asks: if you rebuilt the efficient frontier using a historical window's data and demanded this same return, what weights would the optimiser choose? We do not swap in a historical mean or any "convenient" return to force a solution. As a cross-check, an independent re-computation from our cleaned closes gives a figure within about 0.3 percentage points of each (A ≈ 14.1–14.7%, B ≈ −9.5% to −9.3% depending on the base-date convention); the small gap comes from split-factor inference and conventions, so the supplied values are used unchanged.

DISCLOSURE · CALM WINDOW OVERLAPS THE TARGET WINDOWThe Calm period (10 Sep – 5 Dec 2025) overlaps the one-year target-measurement window (1 Oct 2025 – 30 Sep 2026) for 45 of its 60 trading days (1 Oct – 5 Dec 2025). The "calm" test is therefore partly looking at the same data that produced the target, so it is a calm recent regime rather than an independent one. We have not moved either period.

Why ES matters: VaR tells us where the bad tail begins. ES tells us how painful that tail actually is. For this project, both are translated into ₹ against the ₹15 lakh starting investment.

Which VaR method did we use — and why?

Parametric (variance–covariance) VaR assumes returns follow a bell curve: VaR = −(μ + z·σ) with z = −1.645 at 95%. Historical simulation assumes no shape: sort what actually happened and read the 5th percentile. The choice depends on whether the data is actually bell-shaped, so we tested it.

DECISION · HISTORICAL SIMULATIONOver 2020–26 both portfolios have excess kurtosis of 13.7 and 16.7 (a bell curve has 0): extreme days are far more common than a normal model predicts. In the crash the Jarque–Bera test rejects normality for both portfolios and the parametric ES is lower than the actual average tail loss (A: 10.9% vs 12.5%; B: 6.8% vs 7.8%), so a normal model underestimates the very losses we care about. In calm markets normality is not rejected and both methods agree. A risk measure must work in the crash, so the headline VaR and ES use Historical Simulation; parametric is shown on each side as a cross-check.

What does "VaR at 95%" actually mean?

95% VaR · the 5% tail

The loss that is exceeded on 1 day in 20. On the other 19 days the loss is smaller. In 45 crisis days we expect ~2 exceedances; in 60 calm days, ~3. It is a threshold, not a worst case.

99% VaR · the 1% tail

Exceeded on 1 day in 100. In 45 days we expect 0.45 such days — i.e. the sample usually contains one, which becomes the estimate. Hence 99% historical VaR = the single worst day: informative about the sample, not a stable forecast.

Expected Shortfall

The average loss on the days beyond VaR. VaR says where the bad tail starts; ES says how bad it is once inside. ES ≥ VaR always, and the gap widens when tails are fat.

All figures are 1-day horizon, loss-positive: "VaR 8.41%" means a loss of 8.41% of the portfolio. The ₹ figures multiply by the capital in the box above (default ₹15,00,000).

VaR and ES — three methods, two confidence levels, four portfolio-regime combinations

How each method works

Historical: sort the actual daily portfolio returns, VaR = −PERCENTILE(returns, 1−α) (Excel PERCENTILE.INC), ES = −AVERAGEIF(returns ≤ that percentile). No shape assumed.
Parametric: assume Normal(μ, σ) with the sample mean and STDEV.S: VaR = −(μ + zσ), ES = −(μ − σ·φ(z)/(1−α)).
Monte Carlo: draw simulated daily returns from the same Normal and read the percentile/tail average of the simulated sample.

Monte Carlo set-up (documented)

Model: Gaussian, r ~ N(μ̂, σ̂) with μ̂, σ̂ = each portfolio-window's own sample mean and STDEV.S · simulations: 100,000 per combination · random seed: 20260930 (NumPy default_rng, reproducible) · levels 95% and 99% · horizon 1 day · loss-positive. Simulated results are model output, not observations: they can only reproduce what the Normal assumption implies, and the small gaps to parametric are sampling noise.

SMALL-SAMPLE WARNINGThe Crisis window has only 45 daily observations (Calm: 60). The 99% historical VaR and ES rest on one day, so they are highly unstable and should not be read as robust estimates of extreme tail risk. Even the 95% historical tail is only 3 days. Treat crisis figures as descriptive of this episode, not as forecasts.

Returns: cumulative vs annualised — not interchangeable

Cumulative compounded return R = Π(1+rp,t) − 1 is what an investor actually earned in the window. Annualised arithmetic return = 252 × mean daily return extrapolates a 45- or 60-day average to a year; in the crash it exaggerates (−193% is not a possible loss for a long-only holder). The Markowitz section uses the annualised arithmetic convention for μ and Σ throughout, as in the class workbook.

Equal weights are a choice, not an optimum. For the same return, can a different mix of the same 10 stocks carry less risk — and would that mix change if rebuilt on a crisis or a calm period? We answer it exactly as in Excel Solver: set the objective to the portfolio variance wᵀΣw, minimise it by changing the 10 weights, subject to the constraints below. There are four separate frontiers: A and B, each in Crisis and Calm.

Solver set-up (class convention)
Set objective: σ²ₚ = wᵀΣw (MMULT)
To: Min
By changing: w₁…w₁₀
Subject to: Σwᵢ = 1 · wᵀμ = target return · wᵢ ≥ 1% (class minimum weight) · wᵢ ≤ 100% · no short-selling

The 1% floor means every stock must stay in the portfolio — the optimiser can shrink a stock to 1%, never delete it.

Inputs we feed it

μ = mean daily return × 252 and Σ = sample covariance (÷ N−1) × 252, estimated from the stocks' daily returns inside each window; the Steps 1–8 build on page 2 shows how Σ is made. The target is the supplied current return of each portfolio: A = 14.4221%, B = −9.4474%. Weights were solved with a sequential quadratic programming solver (SciPy SLSQP, tolerance 1e-14), cross-checked against a second algorithm, and then re-validated on every row (see Validation).

Step 0 · Feasibility — can today's return be achieved at all?

Before any optimiser runs we ask what returns are reachable. With a 1% floor on every stock, the lowest reachable return puts the 1% minimum in all stocks and the remaining 90% in the worst stock; the highest puts the 90% in the best stock:

Rmin = 1% × Σμᵢ + (1 − 10 × 1%) × min(μᵢ)  ·  Rmax = 1% × Σμᵢ + (1 − 10 × 1%) × max(μᵢ)

If the target is outside [Rmin, Rmax] we stop: we do not force an optimiser to return a "solution" that violates the constraints, and we do not present weights from a failed run.

Step 5 · What the allocation tells us

RISK–RETURN TRADE-OFFThe minimum-variance portfolio is not the highest-return portfolio: it is the point of lowest risk on the frontier. Moving up the frontier buys return only by taking more volatility (and concentrating in fewer stocks). Targets below the minimum-variance return are inefficient — the same or less return for more risk. In this experiment every feasible target lies on that inefficient side, which is the sharpest form of the finding: the portfolio's current return could be matched or beaten with less risk simply by re-weighting.

Beta, Sharpe ratio and the rank test

Two extra lenses from the course. Beta = cov(rp, rNifty) ÷ var(rNifty): how many % the portfolio moved for a 1% move in the Nifty 50. Sharpe ratio = (annualised return − risk-free rate) ÷ annualised volatility: reward per unit of total risk. Read me, data and checks

Objective & sources

Test whether risk labels and efficient allocations survive a change of market regime. Data: NSE daily files supplied in Daily data.zip (daily CLOSE, EQ series, 1 Jan 2020 – 30 Sep 2026). Methodology: Financial Risk Analytics class materials — the 10-asset VaR/Markowitz workbook, the VaR/ES and backtest files, and the course's interactive reference pages for Monte Carlo VaR, Parametric VaR (matrix, 10 stocks) and the Markowitz frontier. Targets: current_targets.csv.

Conventions

Simple daily return r = Pt/Pt−1 − 1 · portfolio return rp = Σwᵢrᵢ (fixed equal weights, daily rebalanced) · annualised arithmetic return = 252 × mean · annual volatility = √252 × σ · covariance divisor N−1 · VaR/ES are 1-day, loss-positive · percentile = Excel PERCENTILE.INC · tolerances: Σw within 1e-9, achieved vs target return within 1e-7 · beta = cov(rp, rNifty) / var(rNifty) · Sharpe = (252 × mean − rf) / (√252 × σ), rf = repo-rate proxy.

Data audit

Checked per file: only the EQ (equity) series is kept — other series codes (bonds, block deals) appear in NTPC, SBIN and SHRIRAMFIN and were removed; no duplicate dates; no missing or non-numeric closes after that filter; all 20 selected stocks share one 1,677-day trading calendar (so 1,676 daily returns); every stock maps to exactly one portfolio (see ticker list below).

Corporate-action adjustment (split / bonus)

Prices in the files are unadjusted, so a split shows up as a fake −50% to −90% "return". For each event the earlier prices were divided by the one-day price ratio, which sets that day's return to zero. Ratios close to round numbers are almost certainly splits/bonuses; the POWERGRID, SHRIRAMFIN and TRENT factors are inferred rather than confirmed. None of the ten events falls inside the Crisis or Calm windows, so the VaR/ES and frontier results are unaffected; they only touch full-sample statistics used for stock selection.

Market index (Nifty 50) and the risk-free rate

Source: NIFTY 50 one-minute candles from the open GitHub repository technovusin/nifty50-historical-data, 2020–Sep 2026. Daily close proxy: the mean of the last 30 one-minute closes (15:00–15:29) of each day. NSE computes the official closing value from closing prices that are themselves averages over the last 30 minutes, so this reproduces official closes far better than the last tick (mean error 0.012% vs 0.075% on the checks below). Alignment: the index and the 20 stocks share the same calendar; the two Muhurat-trading dates missing from the index file (14 Nov 2020, 24 Oct 2022) are simply dropped from full-period beta pairs and fall outside both windows. Window betas use the same 45 / 60 daily returns as every other calculation.

Official closes: Capital Market / Arihant Capital and Chola Securities daily market notes (Dec 2025 rows) and NSE (23 Mar 2020). Index returns are price returns (no dividends). Risk-free rate: an RBI repo-rate proxy (≈5.0% crisis, 5.50% calm), editable on page 04; a 91-day T-bill yield is the usual alternative.

Limitations

① Selection circularity: stocks were ranked on full-sample volatility that includes the test windows, so the labels are partly hindsight. ② Survivorship: a 30-stock universe of today's large caps, not the full Nifty 200. ③ Small samples: 45 (crisis) and 60 (calm) observations make μ and Σ noisy, so optimal weights are unstable; 99% historical VaR/ES in each window is based on a single day. ④ Calm-window overlap: 45 of its 60 days sit inside the target-measurement window. ⑤ Annualisation: 252 × a short-window mean is an arithmetic extrapolation, not a forecast. ⑥ Close prices only, no dividends; three split factors inferred. ⑦ Targets: supplied values used as given (our own re-computation differs by ≈0.3 pp). ⑧ Monte Carlo is Gaussian by construction and therefore cannot show fat tails. ⑨ Constant equal weights assumed (daily rebalancing). ⑩ No backtest of VaR exceptions was run; the class backtest workbook is a natural extension. ⑪ Beta and Sharpe rest on 45 / 60 days, so they are noisy (calm-window betas especially); the index is an unofficial minute-data feed converted to a daily close proxy, and index returns exclude dividends. ⑫ Risk-free rate is a repo-rate proxy, not a T-bill yield.
